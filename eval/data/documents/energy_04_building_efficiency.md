# Building Energy Efficiency

Building energy use can often be reduced through a combination of insulation, efficient equipment, controls, and occupant behavior. The best improvement depends on climate, building age, operating hours, and existing equipment.

A building management system can schedule heating, cooling, ventilation, and lighting. Sensors provide temperature, occupancy, and air-quality signals, but poor calibration can lead to uncomfortable spaces or unnecessary energy use.

Projects should establish a baseline before changes are made. Savings should be adjusted for weather and occupancy so that a mild season or an empty building is not mistaken for a technology benefit.

Common improvements include sealing air leaks, improving insulation, replacing inefficient lighting, tuning heating and cooling schedules, and maintaining filters and equipment. Measures interact: a tighter building may require ventilation changes, and efficient lighting may reduce internal heat that previously helped warm a room.

An evaluation should compare energy use against comfort, indoor air quality, capital cost, and maintenance effort. Occupants should be included because a control system that saves energy but is difficult to use may be overridden. A small pilot with before-and-after measurements can reveal operational problems before a building-wide rollout.
