# Container Gardening

Container gardens are useful when ground soil is unavailable or mobility is limited. Containers need drainage holes, a growing medium suited to the plant, and a location with enough light.

Small containers dry out faster than large ones. Checking moisture below the surface is more reliable than watering on a fixed schedule. A saucer can protect a surface but should not hold standing water for long periods.

Plants in containers eventually use available nutrients, so a light feeding plan may help. Choose plants with similar water and light needs for the same container, and leave enough room for mature roots.

Container material changes temperature and evaporation. Dark plastic can heat quickly in strong sun, while unglazed clay loses moisture through its sides. A larger container generally buffers temperature and watering mistakes, but it also becomes heavier and harder to move. Stable placement is important on balconies and near walkways.

The growing medium should not usually be ordinary garden soil by itself because it can drain poorly in a pot. A mix with air space, water-holding capacity, and structural stability gives roots better conditions. Drainage holes should remain open, and a layer of gravel at the bottom does not fix a blocked hole or poor medium.
