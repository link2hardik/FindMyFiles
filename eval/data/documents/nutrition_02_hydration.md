# Hydration and Daily Habits

Hydration needs vary with body size, activity, climate, diet, and illness. Water is the main source for most people, while foods such as fruit, soup, and vegetables also contribute fluid.

Thirst, urine color, and changes in activity can provide practical signals, although no single signal is perfect. During prolonged exercise or heavy sweating, electrolytes and a planned drinking schedule may matter more than drinking only when thirsty.

Excessive water intake can also be harmful. People with medical conditions affecting the heart, kidneys, or fluid balance should follow advice from their healthcare professional rather than adopting a universal target.

Fluid losses are increased by heat, exercise, fever, vomiting, diarrhea, and some medications. During short everyday activities, ordinary meals and drinking to thirst are often sufficient. Longer endurance activities require more planning because both dehydration and excessive plain-water intake can cause problems.

Hydration advice should distinguish water from electrolytes and should avoid presenting a single number as appropriate for every person. A useful plan considers the activity duration, environmental conditions, access to fluids, and signs that require medical attention. The purpose of a plan is to support normal function, not to encourage constant drinking without need.
